Lifting for Runners

Kairos builds your running. This is the blueprint for the lifting that supports it — how to think about it, and why. There are no prescribed exercises or set-and-rep tables here on purpose: the principles below travel with you to any gym, and once you understand them you can build the session yourself.

1. Why lift at all

You are not lifting to get bigger. For a runner, added mass you have to carry 26.2 miles is a cost, not a benefit. You are lifting for two things: durability and economy.

Durability is the obvious one. Running is thousands of repetitions of the same small movement, and the tissues that fail under that load — tendon, bone, connective tissue — respond to heavy, deliberate loading in a way they do not respond to more mileage. Most recurring running injuries are capacity problems. Strength work raises capacity.

Economy is the less obvious one. Heavy strength training improves how much oxygen you burn at a given pace, largely through better force production and stiffer, more elastic tendons returning energy on each stride. This is why the effect shows up without meaningful weight gain — you are changing how well you use force, not how much muscle you carry.

The governing constraint: you have one recovery budget, and running already spends most of it. Every decision that follows — how much, how hard, which day — comes back to that single fact. Lifting that compromises your running has failed at its job, no matter how good the session felt.

2. The movement types

Think in movement patterns rather than exercises. There are only a handful, and a balanced week touches all of them. Which specific exercise you use for a pattern matters far less than whether the pattern is covered at all — pick what your gym has, what your body tolerates, and what you can load progressively.

Squat knee-dominant lower body
Bending at the knee and hip together under load. Trains the quadriceps primarily. Why it's here: quad strength is what controls your knee on every landing and every downhill. e.g. back squat, leg press, split squat
Hinge hip-dominant lower body
Pushing the hips back with a relatively fixed knee. Trains the hamstrings, glutes and lower back. Why it's here: this is the most important pattern for a runner. The posterior chain drives propulsion, and hamstring weakness relative to quad strength is one of the better-documented injury risk factors in running. e.g. Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, leg curl
Horizontal push upper body
Pressing away from the chest. Why it's here: balances the pulling work and maintains upper-body structure. Not a performance driver for running — include it for balance, not because it will make you faster. e.g. bench press, dumbbell press, push-up
Vertical push upper body
Pressing overhead. Trains the shoulders and triceps. Why it's here: shoulder health and posture, both of which degrade under high mileage and desk work. e.g. overhead press, landmine press
Horizontal pull upper body
Rowing toward the torso. Trains the mid-back. Why it's here: arm drive and postural endurance late in a race, when form breaks down first in the upper back. e.g. any row variation
Vertical pull upper body
Pulling down from overhead. Trains the lats and biceps. Why it's here: completes upper-body balance against the pressing patterns. e.g. pull-up, lat pulldown
Carry & core trunk
Resisting movement rather than creating it — resisting rotation, resisting side-bending, holding position under load. Why it's here: your trunk's job while running is to stay stiff and transfer force between hips and shoulders. Train it the way it works: by resisting, not by crunching. e.g. loaded carry, plank variation, anti-rotation press

Two runner-specific additions worth deliberate attention, because general programs under-serve them. Calves and lower leg: the soleus absorbs several times body weight per stride and is chronically undertrained — direct calf work, both straight-leg and bent-knee, pays off. Single-leg work: running is a single-leg sport. Split squats, step-ups and single-leg hinges expose side-to-side imbalances that a barbell lets you hide.

3. Training intent

The same movement trains different qualities depending on how you load it. There are three broad intents, and they are not equally useful to you.

Strength

Heavy load, low reps, long rest, high effort per set.

Produces the most force adaptation for the least total fatigue — few reps means little total work, even though each rep is hard. Recovers quickly.

Best default for a runner in a training block.

Hypertrophy

Moderate load, moderate reps, more total sets.

Builds muscle size. More total work means meaningfully more fatigue and soreness, which competes directly with running.

Useful in the off-season or a base phase. Costly mid-block.

Muscular endurance

Light load, high reps, short rest.

The quality you already develop every time you run. Adding more of it in the gym buys you very little and still costs recovery.

Largely redundant. Skip it.

The practical version: lift heavy, keep the volume modest, and let running be your conditioning. The instinct to make lifting sessions feel like a workout — breathless, high-rep, minimal rest — is the single most common way runners get the trade-off backwards, taking on the fatigue cost of hypertrophy work while getting the weakest adaptation.

4. Weekly volume — the number that actually matters

If you take one thing from this document, take this: the primary variable is how many hard sets each muscle group gets per week. Not which exercises you chose. Not the order. Not the machine.

A hard set means a set taken close enough to failure to be a real stimulus. Count them per muscle group, across the whole week, and count compound movements toward their primary movers — a row is back volume, a squat is quad volume.

Weekly hard sets per muscleWhat it does
2–4Maintains what you have. A legitimate goal during a heavy running block or a taper.
6–12The productive range for most people. This is the target for a normal training week.
15+Diminishing returns for a runner, and the recovery cost lands on your running first.

Two useful consequences. First, you can hit a productive week in surprisingly little time — six to ten hard sets per muscle, spread over two or three sessions, is not a lot of gym time. Second, this is how you audit your own program. Add up a week. If a muscle group is at zero, no amount of good exercise selection elsewhere fixes that. Missing volume is invisible until you count.

5. How hard is hard enough

The useful measure is reps in reserve — how many more you could have done with good form when you racked it. Two in reserve means you stopped with two clean reps left.

Aim for one to three in reserve on most sets. That is close enough to failure to drive adaptation and far enough from it to keep the recovery cost sane. Training to true failure raises fatigue sharply for very little extra stimulus, and as a runner you are paying that bill twice — once in the gym and again on tomorrow's run.

The effort cue: hard but crisp. Every rep should look like the first one. When bar speed drops noticeably or form starts to change, the set is over — that is the signal, not the rep count on the page.

6. Splits, and what balance means

A split is just how you distribute the movement patterns across the week. The right one depends almost entirely on how many days you actually lift.

Full body
Every session touches most patterns — a squat or hinge, a push, a pull, some trunk work. Each muscle gets trained often, so a missed session costs you less. Best at two to three days a week, which is where most runners should live.
Upper / lower
Alternates upper-body and lower-body sessions. Concentrates leg work into specific days, which makes it much easier to place around your hard running days. Best at four days (two of each). At three days it works, but one region gets trained twice and the other once — rotate which one across weeks so it evens out.
Push / pull / legs
Splits by function. To train each muscle twice weekly it needs six sessions, which is almost always too much alongside a running plan. At three days each muscle is trained once a week — workable, but you are choosing a structure that needs six days and only giving it three. Rarely the right choice for a runner.

Balance means three things, and they are easy to check:

  • Push against pull. Roughly equal weekly volume. Pressing is more fun and more visible, so it drifts upward; shoulders pay for it.
  • Knee-dominant against hip-dominant. Squat patterns against hinge patterns. Quads dominate by default because squats feel like the "real" leg exercise. For a runner the hinge deserves at least equal billing.
  • Nothing at zero. The muscles that quietly vanish from self-built programs are hamstrings, rear delts, calves and the trunk. None are glamorous. All of them matter more to you than your bench press.

7. Where to start, by days per week

Find your row. These are starting structures, not prescriptions — adjust once you know how you respond.

2 days

Full body, both days.

Cover a squat pattern, a hinge, a push, a pull and some trunk work in each session. Two well-built full-body days is genuinely enough to get most of the durability and economy benefit. This is the sweet spot for most runners in a hard training block — do not assume more is better.

3 days

Full body ×3, or upper / lower / full.

Enough frequency to push weekly volume into the productive range without long sessions. If you go upper/lower/full, put the full-body day last and keep it the lightest of the three.

4 days

Upper / lower, twice each.

The cleanest structure available. Legs are concentrated into two days, which makes them easy to keep away from your long run and your quality sessions. Realistic only in a base phase or lower-mileage period.

5+ days

Upper / lower plus a focused day.

Be honest about the trade-off. Five lifting days alongside five or six running days is a lot of stress for someone whose primary goal is a race result. If your running is going backwards, this is the first thing to cut.

When time is short, cut in this order: isolation work first, then upper-body volume, then upper-body frequency. Protect the hinge, the squat and the single-leg work — those are the ones doing the job you came for.

8. Fitting it around your run week

This is the part general lifting advice cannot give you, because it requires knowing what your running week looks like. You have that above — use it.

There are two defensible philosophies, and the choice is genuinely yours:

Stack — hard days hard

Lift on the days you already run hard. This concentrates stress into fewer days and leaves your easy days genuinely easy, which is the whole point of an easy day. Run first, lift after.

Suits experienced athletes who recover well.

Stagger — spread the stress

Lift on easy or rest days, and never the day before a hard session. Each individual day is more manageable, at the cost of having no truly easy days.

Suits newer athletes and anyone with a history of overuse injury.

Whichever you choose, four rules hold:

  • No heavy legs the day before your long run. The single most reliable way to ruin the most important session of your week.
  • Run first when you share a day. Your race is a running race. The quality session that matters gets your freshest legs.
  • Taper the lifting too. As running volume drops into race week, cut lifting volume alongside it — keep the movements, drop the sets and the effort. You are preserving what you built, not adding to it.
  • Nothing in race week. There is no strength adaptation available in seven days, and there is plenty of fatigue available. Show up fresh.

To place your sessions: find your long run and your quality days on the schedule above, mark the day before each as protected, and put your lifting in what remains — or directly onto the quality days themselves if you are stacking.

9. Knowing when to add weight

Progression should be automatic, not a decision you make session to session. The simplest reliable method is double progression: pick a rep range and work inside it.

When you can hit the top of that range on every set while still leaving your target reps in reserve, add weight next session and drop back to the bottom of the range. Then climb again. That is the whole system — two variables, one at a time, no spreadsheet.

Two expectations worth setting. You will not add weight every session, and trying to is how form degrades. And during a heavy running block, holding your numbers steady is a win — you are asking your body to build two things at once with one recovery budget. Maintaining strength through a marathon build while your running improves is exactly what success looks like.

Scope

This is general educational material about strength training principles, not individualized programming or medical advice. It cannot account for your injury history, medical conditions, or movement limitations. If you are returning from injury, have a relevant medical condition, or are new to loaded strength training, work with a qualified coach or physical therapist — particularly on technique for loaded movements, where a form check early is worth far more than any program.
